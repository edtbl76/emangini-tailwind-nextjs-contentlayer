Every few months someone asks me which guardrail they should put in front of their model, and the question carries a premise worth pulling out and looking at, because the premise is doing more damage than the answer ever could.

The premise is that there is a _which_. One box, in front of the model, that makes it safe.

I want to be careful about how I disagree, because the obvious rebuttal — _you need four tools, not one_ — is also wrong, and it is wrong in a way that took me a research pass and a falsified assumption to see. This piece is not an argument about how many products to buy. It is an argument that the guardrail problem has seams in it, that several groups working from different directions keep finding those seams in the same places, and that the most interesting thing about the seams is how hard they have been to remove.

It is also a report from the middle rather than the end. There is a measurement that would settle the central question, nobody has published it, and I do not have it either. I would rather say that plainly and tell you where to push than write the version that sounds finished.

---

## The question with a false premise

Start with what the field's own guidance says, because it does not describe a single gate.

OWASP has kept prompt injection at the top of its Top 10 for LLM applications for a second consecutive edition, and its mitigation guidance is explicitly layered: input validation _and_ output filtering _and_ privilege restriction _and_ human approval for sensitive operations. The document states plainly that neither retrieval-augmented generation nor fine-tuning fully mitigates the class (OWASP, 2025). That last sentence is the one people skip. It says the problem is not solvable at the model layer, which means it has to be handled somewhere else, which means there is an architecture question underneath the shopping question.

Beurer-Kellner et al. (2025) arrive at the same shape from the design-pattern direction, proposing six patterns for prompt-injection resistance rather than one, and trading utility against security in each. Fourteen authors across ETH Zurich, Google, Microsoft, IBM, and Invariant Labs, and no single recommendation among them.

So the shopping question has a bad premise. But replacing it with _buy four things instead of one_ just makes a longer shopping list. The useful replacement is a different question entirely: **which jobs is your system doing, and can you name which component owns each one?** That question survives whether you assembled four services or bought one console, and the rest of this piece is about why the jobs turn out to be stable even when the products are not.

## Layer is not tool, and tier is not layer

Three words get used interchangeably in this space and the conflation is load-bearing, so I want to separate them before anything else. I am not being pedantic here; I conflated two of them myself while researching this piece, and it cost me a wrong conclusion I will describe later.

A **layer** is a job: a distinct kind of failure being caught. A **tool** is a product that implements one or more layers. A **tier** is an escalation _inside_ one layer — a cheap check that defers to an expensive one.

These are independent axes. One tool can span several layers. One layer can escalate across several tiers.

My own Classify layer is the clean demonstration. It runs a small safety model on CPU by default and escalates to a larger one on GPU when a heavier verdict is wanted. That is one layer, one tool, two tiers. Meta's own model cards document why the escalation is worth paying for: the 1B variant reports an English F1 of 0.899 against the 8B's 0.939, and Meta recommends the larger model where classification quality matters more than deployment cost (Meta, n.d.-a). Nothing about that escalation makes it a second layer. It is the same job, done harder.

Keep the axes apart and a lot of apparent disagreement in this field dissolves. A vendor bundling three layers into one product has not disproved that there are three jobs. A team running one tool has not necessarily got one layer.

## The four layers, and the one job each does

Here are the four, described by the job rather than the product.

**Scan** is fast input/output sanitization, running on every request at the agent edge and the tool gateway: injection detection, PII handling, grounding, action policy. In my stack this is not a single product but a composition — an injection classifier, a PII engine, and a grounding check, each independently maintained. The reason for that shape is the subject of a later section.

The injection component is a fine-tuned classifier. Meta reports its 86M model at an AUC of .998 and 97.5% recall at a 1% false-positive rate on English, with the 22M variant at .995 and 88.7% for roughly a quarter of the latency (Meta, n.d.-b). Those are vendor-reported figures measured against a private benchmark, and I am citing them as vendor claims rather than independent results. The PII component is Microsoft's Presidio, which separates detection — finding spans and labeling entity types with confidence — from transformation, which replaces, masks, redacts, or encrypts them.

The grounding component is the least settled of the three and I would rather say so than present it as solved. It works on Natural Language Inference: the retrieved context is the premise, the model's answer is the hypothesis, and an answer not entailed by its sources is ungrounded. NLI entailment correlates with attribution and factuality and has become the standard baseline for hallucination detection, with a clear lineage running from SummaC through AlignScore and MiniCheck to purpose-trained factual-consistency models. But Tamber et al. (2025) built an evolving RAG-faithfulness leaderboard and introduced a judge-based evaluator specifically because they observed limitations in exactly this generation of detector. Cheap, fast, and unfinished is the honest description.

**Classify** is model-based content-safety classification: safe or unsafe, plus a category. Inan et al. (2023) built Llama Guard specifically as an LLM-based input-output safeguard with a taxonomy, arguing that classification is a different task from scanning rather than a stronger version of it.

The category is what earns the layer. A worked example from my own stack: given the same dangerous request, the small model binned it as _violent crime_ while the larger one binned it more precisely as _indiscriminate weapons_. Those are S1 and S9 in the MLCommons hazard taxonomy, distinct entries on Meta's published card, where S9 covers chemical, biological, radiological, nuclear, and high-yield explosive weapons specifically (Meta, n.d.-a). A scanner returning an undifferentiated toxicity score cannot make that distinction, and the distinction is the difference between a log line and an incident.

**Dialog** is topical and conversational control: refusing off-domain requests, resisting jailbreaks that build across turns. Rebedea et al. (2023) built NeMo Guardrails around dialogue management rather than filtering, which is the right frame — a stateless scanner has no notion of a conversation to keep in scope, so this is not something Scan could be tuned into doing.

**Structure** is output-schema validation with repair. Guardrails AI implements it as a re-ask: when a field fails validation, it generates a prompt targeting that specific field and asks again, up to a configured retry cap (Guardrails AI, n.d.). It wraps only structured-output producers, where a malformed blob silently corrupts whatever reads it downstream.

One consequence of drawing the lines this way is worth stating, because I got it wrong first. **Content safety belongs in Classify, not Scan.** It is tempting to let the fast scanner own a toxicity detector too, since it is already inspecting every request and the marginal cost looks small. Resist it. Toxicity is a content-policy judgment, and moving it out of Scan removed a redundant model from the hot path and put the judgment where it could be graded by category.

## The citation trail

Now the part I got wrong, and the correction, because the correction is the actual argument.

My first draft of this piece claimed that several groups had arrived at these four layers _independently_, and offered that as evidence the layers were real. That claim does not survive checking, and I want to show the checking rather than quietly drop it.

Dong et al. (2024), a position paper at ICML, examined exactly Llama Guard, NVIDIA NeMo, and Guardrails AI together as the open-source guardrail landscape and concluded that current offerings were incomplete and that a systematic approach toward more complete solutions was needed. That was published in February 2024 — before I assembled anything. The grouping of those particular systems, and the conclusion drawn from the grouping, were in the literature first.

OpenGuardrails (Wang & Li, 2025) is not a separate discovery either. Its related work names Qwen3Guard, LlamaFirewall, Prompt Guard 2, and the OpenAI Moderation API, and its evaluation tables benchmark against Llama Guard 3, Llama Guard 4, WildGuard, ShieldGemma, NemoGuard, and PolyGuard. It positions itself as advancing beyond prior efforts that "open-source either models or rule-based tools in isolation." That is a paper in direct conversation with the same two systems occupying my Classify and Dialog layers. It is the next move in one conversation, not a parallel one.

And Amazon Bedrock Guardrails, examined against its own component documentation, offers content filters, prompt-attack detection, sensitive-information filters, contextual grounding checks, denied topics, word filters, and automated reasoning checks — each a separately configurable policy (Amazon Web Services, n.d.). Map those onto the four jobs and the overlap is close to complete: content filters are Classify, prompt attacks and PII and grounding are Scan, and denied topics is Dialog shipping as a checkbox.

So this is one field converging in public, and the trail is traceable. That is a weaker claim than independent invention.

It is also, I have come to think, a better one — for a reason that is the subject of the next section.

## The seams that would not merge

Independent invention would have been the more impressive story and the less useful evidence, because I could never have demonstrated it. You cannot prove that three groups did not read each other. What the trail shows instead is checkable by anyone willing to open the papers.

Here is what it shows. Parties who read each other are _free to collapse the boundaries_. Nothing obliges a team building a guardrail system in 2025 to preserve the seams a 2024 position paper described. They could merge them, and one of them explicitly set out to.

OpenGuardrails is a unification project by its own description: a single platform covering content-safety detection, manipulation defense, and data protection, positioned against prior work that shipped models or rules in isolation. It had every incentive to dissolve the distinctions. What it actually did was merge Scan and Classify into one model while still treating content safety and model-manipulation detection as separate detection jobs — and then drop Dialog and Structure rather than absorb them. The boundaries survived the merger as internal structure. (I should note the limits of my own reading here: I am working from the paper's abstract, related work, and evaluation tables rather than a full implementation review.)

Bedrock is the same story from the opposite direction. AWS had every commercial reason to sell one thing called "guardrails" and instead shipped seven separately configurable policies with distinct detection methods, tuning surfaces, and failure modes. A vendor rebuilding this from scratch, under pressure to make it simple to buy, kept the seams.

That is the argument. **Seams that survive a deliberate attempt to remove them are hard seams.** Two groups with strong incentives to unify these functions — one an explicit unification project, one a hyperscaler optimizing for a purchasable product — preserved the boundaries anyway. That is the strongest evidence I can offer that the seams belong to the problem rather than to anyone's product, and it is evidence you can check without taking my word for anything.

## Structure is the layer nobody sells you

Run the mapping across all three bodies and one row comes out different.

Scan appears everywhere. Classify appears everywhere. Dialog appears in Bedrock as denied topics and is absent from OpenGuardrails. **Structure is absent or partial in every external body examined.** Bedrock's automated reasoning checks validate whether a response complies with logical policies expressed in natural language, which is genuinely useful and is not schema conformance; there is no documented re-ask repair loop. OpenGuardrails does not claim the job at all.

The interesting question is not that the market skipped a layer. It is _why this particular layer_, and I think the answer is one I have argued before in a different context.

In writing about harness engineering, I argued that what you can buy is the inexpensive, transferable artifact, while what actually creates value is local and cannot be bought at any scale — the devil is in the details, and the details are yours alone (Mangini, 2026). Structure is that argument instantiated in guardrails, and it explains the gap in the table rather than merely restating it.

Scan, Classify, and Dialog are all sellable because their subject matter _generalizes_. Harmful content is harmful for every customer. Injection looks like injection everywhere. Even denied topics generalize to a mechanism — a vendor ships the list-shaped hole and you fill it in.

A schema contract does not generalize. It is defined entirely by which of your producers emits it and which of your consumers breaks when it is malformed. There is no version of that a vendor can ship you, because the thing being validated is the seam between two components that only exist in your system. So the reader who bought three layers cannot buy the fourth — not because no vendor has gotten around to it, but because it is not a buyable kind of thing.

Which produces the practical finding I would most want a reader to take away. If you are running on a managed platform, **you probably already have three of these layers and may not know they are three.** Your problem is not that you lack guardrails. It is that you cannot name which one failed when something gets through, and the one you are genuinely missing is the one no purchase order will fix.

## Every layer is individually unreliable

I have now spent several sections arguing that these four layers are real. Here is the evidence that should temper what you do with that, and I would want to read it before adopting any of the above.

**They are evadable.** Hackett et al. (2025) tested six production protection systems, including Meta's Prompt Guard and Microsoft's Azure Prompt Shield, using character-injection techniques and adversarial machine-learning evasion. They report up to 100% evasion success while preserving the attack's utility, and show that attackers can compute word-importance rankings against offline white-box models and transfer them to black-box targets without access to the deployed system. Two honest caveats: that figure is a maximum across conditions rather than a uniform result, and the work predates the current generation of Meta's injection classifier, so it is evidence about the class of detector rather than a verdict on any specific current model.

**They are context-fragile.** She et al. (2025) tested three Llama Guard models and two GPT-oss models and found that inserting _benign_ documents into the guardrail's context flips its judgment in roughly 11% of input-guardrail cases and 8% of output cases. Tested mitigations produced only minor improvements. They name this a context-robustness gap, and it lands directly on any stack whose Scan layer sits in front of retrieval-augmented requests — which is to say, the exact context that degrades the verdict.

**They are badly calibrated, in both directions.** Huang et al. (2025) ran 1,123 prompts across three major platforms and found false-positive rates spanning two orders of magnitude, from 0.1% to 13.1%. On the strictest platform, 131 benign prompts were blocked, and they were disproportionately _code review and mathematics questions_ — the filter could not separate benign code-shaped text from exploit-shaped text. Meanwhile one platform's input filters caught only 53% of malicious prompts where others caught 91–92%, and role-play framing bypassed filters across every system tested.

Tighten the guard and you block your own engineers asking for a code review. Loosen it and you let attacks through. There is no setting that escapes the trade; there is only measuring where you actually sit on it.

I want to be careful about what this evidence does and does not do to the argument. It does not undermine the layer decomposition — if anything, unreliable individual detectors are the entire case for composing them. But it cuts the other way too, and the piece would be dishonest if it only took the flattering reading. Defense in depth assumes failures are _uncorrelated_. If every layer degrades under the same retrieved-document context, the failures are correlated, and stacking buys less than the diagram suggests. That is a real hole and I do not have a measurement that closes it.

## Engineering realities

Three things I would want told to me before starting.

**Fail open, everywhere.** Every guard should degrade to "not guarded," never to "no answer." A guard outage must not take a response offline. Verdicts ship in shadow mode — recorded, not enforced — and you measure the false-positive rate on real traffic before promoting anything to a hard block. Given the two-orders-of-magnitude spread Huang et al. found, shipping straight to enforcement means choosing a false-positive rate blind. I will flag that fail-open is a considered position rather than a universal one: it is right for an assistant surface, and a product with a genuine duty of care to vulnerable users should weigh it differently.

**Packaging dictates deployment.** Classify, Structure, and Dialog could not co-install with the surrounding data and ML stack in my case — Guardrails AI pinned a `click` version irreconcilable with common orchestration tooling. That is a first-hand observation I was not able to confirm against current upstream pins, so take it as a field report; the general pattern is well documented in the project's own issue tracker. Each component ended up as its own service. That was not a principled decision about isolation and blast radius. It was forced, and I only got to describe it as architecture afterward.

**The reliable primitive beats the elegant one.** NeMo's signature contribution is Colang, a domain-specific language for expressing conversational rails, and it is genuinely a nicer way to state the intent. It would not fire for me, across two builds and an earlier standalone trial. The fix was to route topicality through the `self check input` rail instead — an LLM-as-judge check that does fire — with a custom refusal message so user-facing behavior was identical. That fallback is a first-class documented flow rather than a workaround (NVIDIA, n.d.). Be clear about the epistemic status here: that Colang did not fire in my hands is a field report, not a documented defect, and you should read it as one. The transferable lesson is the general one. When an elegant primitive will not cooperate after honest attempts, switching to the proven-but-plainer one is the engineering call, and the DSL you admired is not worth the outage you are shipping to protect it.

## Name layers by job, because the tools churn

My original Scan layer was an all-in-one open-source scanner. It was a good choice and I would defend it: an independent benchmark of thirteen LLM security solutions named it one of the two best tools evaluated on the trade-off between usability and performance (Palit & Woods, 2025).

In July 2026 its maintainers archived the repository. Read-only, no further development, associated models frozen alongside it. The parent company had been acquired the year before and its products folded into a commercial platform; the last release had shipped over a year earlier.

Nothing broke. An MIT-licensed archived scanner runs exactly as well the day after the archive as the day before. But a frozen detection stack ages in a way a frozen JSON parser does not, because injection techniques keep moving and the detector no longer does.

What made the replacement tractable was a property of the architecture rather than any foresight on my part. The layer was defined by its job — sanitize I/O at the edge — not by the product implementing it. So the swap was a _de-bundling_: a dedicated injection classifier for the injection job, Presidio directly for the PII job, a dedicated NLI check for grounding.

And here is the detail I find most instructive, because it happened twice. The retired tool's anonymization scanners had been wrapping Presidio underneath all along. Its factual-consistency scanner was already an NLI-shaped job. Going direct removed a wrapper, not a capability — in both cases. I had been running the replacements for two years without knowing it.

That is what a correctly placed seam looks like from the inside. The bundle came apart along lines that were already there, which is why it came apart cheaply. The four layers have held across the entire life of this system. One of the four tools did not last two years. I no longer read that ratio as bad luck.

## What none of us have measured

Now the objection I cannot answer, which is also the reason this piece exists.

The strongest case against everything above is **path dependence**. The layers may persist not because the problem has these seams, but because everyone inherited the same 2024 framing. Dong et al. grouped Llama Guard, NeMo, and Guardrails AI; every subsequent system has been answering that grouping. Under this reading the seams are a convention the field locked in early, and their persistence measures the strength of the convention rather than the structure of the problem.

This objection is sharper than the independence question it replaced, because the citation trail — which is my evidence — is _also_ the mechanism by which lock-in would propagate. The same trail supports both readings. I cannot separate them by reading more papers.

What would separate them is a measurement, and the two readings predict different results. If the boundaries are conventional, the layers should substantially co-fire — the content classifier flagging traffic the scanner already caught. If they track genuinely distinct failure modes, they should not.

**No such measurement is published.** I went looking specifically for it. There is component ablation _within_ single systems, and there is adjacent work on over-refusal, but I could find nothing measuring cross-layer overlap in a composed production stack. That is a gap in the public record, not just in my notes, and it is the single most useful thing anyone reading this could contribute.

There is a second open question I am leaving open rather than papering over. Huang et al. observed that model alignment independently blocked harmful outputs in most cases where input filters failed — which is direct support for the rival position that a competent model plus one scanner covers most real risk. I went looking for a verifiable source testing whether external guardrails add measurable value over a safety-aligned model and did not find one I could stand behind. I had a survey that made the argument; it sat behind a paywall and I had read only its abstract, so I cut it rather than cite something I had not read. That leaves the objection unrebutted on the record. I would rather have the gap than the citation.

---

## A report from mid-stream

So this is not a recommendation, and I would resist reading it as one.

What I can defend: the guardrail problem has at least four separable jobs; several groups working in public keep finding them in the same places; the groups that tried to merge them kept the boundaries anyway; one of those jobs cannot be bought because it is irreducibly local to your system; each layer individually is evadable, fragile, and poorly calibrated; and the tools implementing them churn faster than the jobs do.

What I cannot defend: that any of this is more than a well-entrenched convention. The measurement that would tell us is not in the literature, and the honest position is that a field can converge on a wrong decomposition and stay converged for years.

If you have shadow-mode logs across more than one of these layers, you are holding the thing nobody has published. What fraction of what your classifier flags did your scanner already catch? If the answer is most of it, I am wrong in an interesting way and I would like to know. If it is very little, the seams are real and we can stop arguing from citation trails.

Come diverge. That is more useful to me than agreement, and it is the only way this gets settled.
